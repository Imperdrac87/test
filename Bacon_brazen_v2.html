The Legend of the Brazen Head

In the folklore of late medieval Oxford, immortalized in Robert Greene's 1589 play Friar Bacon and Friar Bungay, Roger Bacon engineered an artificial head of solid brass.

"With demonic pacts and seven years of unremitting mechanical labor, Bacon sought to imbue the head with speech, so that it might teach them how to encompass all of England with an impenetrable rampart of solid brass."

The spirits swore the head would speak only once within a 30-day vigil, but the precise hour remained concealed. Exhausted after days without sleep, Bacon left his servant Miles on watch, bidding him under pain of death to awaken his master the instant the head uttered a sound.

In the dead of night, the head awakened and proclaimed: "Time is." Miles, deeming two words unworthy of waking the master, laughed. Half an hour later: "Time was." Miles again scoffed. A third time it cried out: "Time is past!"

Instantaneously, a bolt of lightning flashed through the chamber, a demonic hand appeared with a hammer, and the Brazen Head shattered upon the stone flags. Bacon awoke only to discover that his lifetime's crowning endeavor had been reduced to dust.
